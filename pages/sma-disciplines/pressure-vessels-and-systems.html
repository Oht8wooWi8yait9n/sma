Pressure Systems

There are approximately 10,000 ground-based Pressure Vessels and Pressurized Systems (PVS) across the agency. Nearly all of the systems store enough energy to cause costly damage, serious injury or even fatalities if they are not operated or maintained properly. NASA’s PVS program tracks, inspects and analyzes these systems to ensure their integrity and compliance with applicable requirements, thereby helping to achieve safe and reliable operation.

In addition, NASA uses a wide range of PVS in flight applications, including both metallic and Composite Overwrapped Pressure Vessels (COPVs), piping and valves. These systems typically store gases and liquids that are essential either to the launch process or on-orbit operations. Their reliability is critical, since once these vessels leave the pad it is usually not possible to inspect or service them unless and until they return to Earth. They typically follow a rigorous program of qualification and acceptance testing in order to ensure that mission requirements are met.

Ground-Based Pressure Vessels Safety SMA-OS-WBT-275

This stand-alone course addresses the potential hazards and safety precautions associated with ground-based Pressure Vessels and Systems (PVS). It is designed for Safety and Mission Assurance (SMA) professionals, health/safety personnel and any NASA employee who needs a basic understanding of PVS. The goal is to increase their knowledge of pressure system safety so that they can recognize and report some common potential hazards, thus improving safety and helping to ensure the mission.

Pressure Vessel Code Application Training SMA-HQ-WBT-306

Pressure vessels are used at every NASA center and facility for everything from shop air and potable water to high-pressure gas storage, hypergolic fuels and cryogenic liquids. Although many vessel applications at NASA are similar to those found in other industries, NASA has some unique applications such as high pressure, irregular use, very old equipment and unique equipment that might require different methods of evaluating or applying the American Society of Mechanical Engineers' Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code, Section VIII, Div. 1. Also, the characteristics of the government contracting process make clear, complete and accurate statements of work essential during procurements. This course was developed to cover the application of the code at NASA centers and other NASA facilities. It includes how the code is used to specify, procure, install and evaluate vessels.

NASA Efforts Towards a Holistic COTS Approach for Space Avionics

Print Version

Aerospace electrical hardware systems are required to reliably operate in extremely harsh environments that include, for example, temperature extremes, intense vibrational stress, drastic temperature cycling and high radiation exposure. The nature of aerospace electronics also demands high initial reliability since, with very few exceptions, there is no chance for servicing and repairs. Common risk mitigation techniques for this type of situation are to use electrical components that are regarded as “high reliability” because of additional controls and requirements applied in their design, manufacturing and testing. That methodology has been successful for decades, but limited availability of space-grade MIL-SPEC parts and a need for using the most current technologies in system designs have necessitated the incorporation of commercial electronics into space flight hardware.

This presentation presents the methodology championed at NASA to utilize more commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) electronics. This includes fostering relationships with high-quality manufacturers who are producing very reliable devices in high volume, using automated production equipment and statistical process controls to establish “zero defect” workmanship. After verification of these criteria, these companies are designated as “Industry Leading Parts Manufacturers (ILPMs).” Additional testing may also be needed to ensure the parts meet the project’s Mission, Environment, Application and Lifetime (MEAL), including radiation testing.

NASA SMA Data Management and Analysis of Launch Provider Fleet

Print Version

NASA SMA Data Management and Analysis of Launch Provider Fleet 

As the number of annual US commercial and US government launches increases to over 100 in 2024, NASA Launch Services Program (LSP) Safety and Mission Assurance (SMA) sought new methods to handle and analyze data trends in the launch provider’s fleets. Using off-the-shelf software from the NASA provided Microsoft Suite (Power Automate, PowerBI, and SharePoint) and free data analysis source code from Python, RCode, and Julia the team was able to collect, process, and analyze various launch vehicle databases (non-conformances, design changes, supplier management, risk, manufacturing key characteristics, etc.) to highlight focus areas for the mission assurance team.  

Using Microsoft Power Automate, the team was able to automate the data gathering of hundreds of thousands of data points and provide a method to regularly update data week-to-week. Once data model was established linking data from multiple sources, the team used Microsoft PowerBI to display the information and publish to SharePoint as dynamic dashboards for NASA Mission Assurance team. By manipulating the filters on the dashboard, team members can focus attention in a proactive versus reactive manner by focusing on variations in work centers, site locations, particular manufacturing and testing processes, non-conformance disposition and/or a flight critical component. Armed with the data, NASA personnel can use the time series data and statistics to investigate further into potential problem areas and focus their attention on reducing mission risk. 

In addition to focusing on fleet-wide trends, the team has used the information to improve the internal processes for Hardware Acceptance Review (HAR), Configuration Management (CM) and Supplier Management Review (SMR). With limited personnel resources, the team uses the data collected to selects HAR components specific to the mission.  Using PowerBI and Power Automate, the team can recommend the flight critical components needing review by the mission team based on criteria such as high number of non-conformances, design changes, first flight items, testing anomalies, and/or identified risks. Furthermore, by running the Power Automate scripts multiple times a week the team can monitor the launch vehicle configuration and inform team members when a component has been installed on the vehicle. In turn, associated Work Orders (WOs) and Acceptance Data Packages (ADPs) are ready for review by the assigned mission assurance team member. To monitor the teams progress during the HAR, PowerBI dashboards allow for a quick graphical review by leadership. 

Just like how the improvements in the HAR process focus the team on what to review and when to review it, the improvements in the Supplier Management Review increased team efficiency. Using the data model, the team can narrow down focus on suppliers needing more attention and provide recommendations on the launch provider supplier audits the NASA SMA should participate in throughout the year. What use to be a manual process, the team applied the same criteria established from the Kaizen event held a few years ago to the data model resulting in an automated go-no go decision. The team can focus resources on struggling suppliers and monitor the overall health of the suppliers. 

With the number of launch providers and launched increasing each year, NASA LSP SMA will continue to look for new ways to improve efficiency. Python, RCode, and Juila interface with PowerBI is being used to improve the graphical presentation and interaction for the user as well as assessing the time series for predictive and statistical analysis. For example, using functions within RCode the time series data for the rocket engine inlet pressure or weekly non-conformance tickets can be broken down into trend, seasonality, and randomness components to provide a clearer picture of data and highlight areas of concern. 

Ultimately, NASA LSP SMA is working to improve their internal processes to reduce manual tasks while improving on the decision-making tools. In an environment with strained resources and increasing workloads, the team has found innovative ways to us the NASA standard software tools provided to them in order to increase efficiency of the mission assurance team.

Challenging the role of Quality Engineering in NASA projects

Print Version

Challenging the role of Quality Engineering in NASA projects: Maximizing Our Value and Achieving Industry Alignment 

Traditionally, quality engineering in NASA projects has limited involvement during the early phases of development. This is largely due to the perception that quality engineers are only responsible for inspection and requirements verification, coupled with early-stage budget constraints and a lack of awareness within the engineering and project management communities of the value quality engineers can add. As a result, projects prioritize proof of concept and technology maturation over allocating even minimal resources to early quality engineering, despite its potential for high return on investment. This delayed involvement often forces quality engineers to overcompensate later, addressing risks and missed opportunities that could have been mitigated during product development or supplier selection. 

In contrast, the aerospace industry views quality engineering as integral throughout the entire product lifecycle, starting with new product development. Industry quality engineers take on a broader role, implementing Advanced Product Quality Planning (APQP) tools early in the process to identify and mitigate risks. As projects progress, they also lead continuous improvement efforts and are accountable for process performance metrics during manufacturing, ensuring that quality is embedded from the outset and that lessons learned are continuously applied to achieve zero waste. 

NASA’s current approach often cites the unique, low-volume nature of its product portfolio, implying that broader industry practices are not applicable. However, in an environment of tight budgets and frequent project cancellations, NASA’s quality engineering community must rethink its role in helping projects stay on budget and on schedule. This presentation will examine the benefits of involving quality engineering early for NASA projects. It will also present a case study highlighting pilot metrics designed to measure execution success of inhouse projects. In addition, recommendations to better educate our community on how quality engineering not only drives tangible improvements but also ensures proper recognition for these contributions will be outlined.

Section 5.39

Print Version

5.39 Exceptions for Model Rocket Motors 

5.39.1 Applicability

This section shall apply to the subset of model rocket motors that satisfy all of the following:

  1. Ignited by electrical means using the manufacturer’s intended ignition system(s).
  2. Contain no more than 2.2 ounces (62.5 grams) of propellant material.
  3. Produce less than 17.92 pounds seconds (80 newton-seconds) of total impulse with thrust duration not less than 0.050 second.
  4. Constructed such that all the chemical ingredients are preloaded into a cylindrical paper or similarly constructed nonmetallic tube that will not fragment into sharp, hard pieces.
  5. Designed so they will not burst under normal conditions of use, are incapable of spontaneous ignition, and do not contain any type of explosive or pyrotechnic warhead other than a parachute or recovery-system activation charge.
  6. Used in the manner for which the manufacturer designed it (e.g. only in model rockets, not as an igniter for NASA experimental testing).
  7. Manufactured and certified in accordance with NFPA 1125 chapter 8.

Any model rocket motor that does not satisfy these requirements shall adhere to the requirements for explosives in this standard.

5.39.2 Storage 

5.39.2.1  Model rocket motors shall be treated and stored as Class 4.1 flammable materials in accordance with the packaging requirements in Department of Transportation Special Permit 7887 (DOT-SP 7887).

5.39.2.2  The minimum storage requirements of applicable model rocket motors shall entail placement inside a flammable storage cabinet.  

5.39.2.3  Model rocket motors shall not be stored with ignitors installed.

5.39.3 Licensing

Explosive licensing requirements of this document do not apply.

5.39.4 Transportation

5.39.4.1  Model rocket motors transported by vehicles shall comply with packaging specifications defined by Department of Transportation Special Permit 7887 (DOT-SP 7887).  

5.39.4.2  Transportation of model rocket motors shall be done by an individual of at least 18 years of age trained in the hazards associated with model rocket motors.  

5.39.5 Use

The installation and use of model rocket motors shall conform to all local, state, and federal regulations in addition to the manufacturer’s instructions.

5.39.6 Training and Medical Requirements

When using model rocket motors applicable to section 5.39, individuals shall be exempt from the training and physical requirements of this document.

Probabilistic Risk Assessment (PRA): Analytical Process for Recognizing Design and Operational Risk

SMA-RM-WBT-251
Print Version

This course provides an overview of Probabilistic Risk Assessment (PRA), one of the tools in NASA’s Safety and Mission Assurance Toolbox. PRA is a comprehensive, structured and disciplined approach to identifying and analyzing risk in engineered systems and/or processes. It attempts to quantify rare event probabilities of failures. It attempts to take into account all possible events or influences that could reasonably affect the system or process being studied. It is inherently and philosophically a Bayesian methodology. In general, PRA is a process that seeks answers to three basic questions: 

  1. What kinds of events or scenarios can occur (i.e., what can go wrong)?
  2. What are the likelihoods and associated uncertainties of the events or scenarios?
  3. What consequences could result from these events or scenarios?

Course Number: SMA-RM-WBT-251

Length: 3.00 hours

Audience: This course is intended for Reliability and Maintainability engineers, program and project managers, system design engineers, safety and management managers, and program and  project Chief Safety Officers.

Quality

Print Version

NASA uses both NASA-internal and internationally recognized standard requirements documents that drive higher compliance and lower cost through their technical applicability to NASA’s work, their technical validity, their repeatability and their stability. NPR 8735.2, NASA Hardware Quality Assurance Program Requirements for Programs and Projects, documents NASA’s Quality Assurance technical standards.

NASA’s Office of Safety and Mission Assurance (OSMA) gives particular attention to leading and informing the hardware Quality policy in the following Quality sub-disciplines: Metrology and Calibration; Electrical and Mechanical Systems Workmanship; ESD Control; NDE; and Electrical, Electronic, and Electromechanical (EEE) Parts. These sub-disciplines are led by an OSMA-delegated program manager or a Subject Matter Expert. Typically, the sub-discipline leader heads up an agencywide working group, which represents NASA’s interests within the industry standards voluntary consensus community, and coordinates and recommends NASA Quality standards and/or policies. (See the EEE Parts page for information on OSMA’s program for Quality Assurance policy for EEE parts.)

For mission hardware built by an external supplier, NASA Quality involves Government Contract Quality Assurance (GCQA) activities associated with contract administration. Federal Acquisition Regulations standardize contract clauses and inform contracting officers how to write contracts and then administer them throughout the life of the contract. NASA’s Quality Assurance Subject Matter Experts provide consultation to contract officers to ensure the right Quality requirements are included in the contracts and that the contracts provide for access by NASA Quality personnel to perform pre-acceptance verification of both the likelihood of a supplier to achieve compliance with the requirements and verification of the manufactured or processed items themselves. Product verifications may be performed during manufacturing (i.e., “in process”) or when processing is completed (i.e., “at acceptance”). GCQA requirements are found in NPR 8735.2.
