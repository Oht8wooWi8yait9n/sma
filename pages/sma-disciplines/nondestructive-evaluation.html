Nondestructive Evaluation

Nondestructive Evaluation (NDE) describes a variety of processes used to inspect for discontinuities or differences in characteristics without destroying the usefulness of the part or system. The NDE Development Program is a research and development organization advancing NDE at NASA sites across the nation to assure spacecraft Quality and mission success. Specifically, the program oversees development of inspection techniques requested or anticipated for upcoming missions, trains center NDE managers, develops and updates inspection standards, and supports equipment development and procurement. The NDE Development Program works alongside the NDE Technical Discipline Team, which focuses on addressing immediate mission needs.

Flash Infrared Thermography and NASA Technology Licensing Opportunities

Researchers at the NASA Johnson Space Center developed and refined Flash Infrared Thermography as a means of Nondestructive Evaluation (NDE). NASA’s innovative technology offers three complementary contrast tools that provide characterization of flaws and anomalies in nonmetallic composite structures using flash infrared thermography. The method can detect smaller flaws and provide quantitative and qualitative information about the shape, size, depth and location of anomalies. NASA’s designed methods assist the operator in defining the boundaries of flaws. For industries interested in NDE of composite materials — this technology can be integrated with existing infrared thermography hardware systems. (Video Credit: NASA Johnson)

Don Brandl

Program Executive, Quality Assurance and Mechanical Systems Assurance, NASA Office of Safety and Mission AssurancE

Donald “Don” E. Brandl is the Program Executive for Quality Assurance and Mechanical Systems Assurance for the NASA Office of Safety and Mission Assurance (OSMA). 

Brandl began his career as a hardware engineer for Lockheed Martin where he worked multiple Department of Defense (DoD) programs developing Automated Test Equipment (ATE) for the Navy and Army. He oversaw prototype development, design, and ATE testing and demonstrations to DoD customers.

Beginning in 2001, Brandl worked on the Space Shuttle Program as a software test engineer for United Space Alliance, performing Independent Verification and Validation (IV&V) on newly developed or modified software for the Space Shuttle Program Launch Processing Systems. He performed IV&V for the Record and Playback Subsystem, Firing Room Checkout Control and Monitor System, and Shuttle Connector Analysis Network. Brandl later moved into performing Reliability analyses for ground operations equipment in support of the Space Shuttle Program.

Brandl began working for NASA in 2006 and spent three years as the lead of the Safety Engineering Branch for the Kennedy Space Center SMA Division in support of the Launch Services Program (LSP). In this position, he provided leadership and technical expertise to a team of System Safety engineers and operations safety specialist personnel responsible for assessing launch vehicle design, spacecraft design and launch site operations. The team was also responsible for ensuring accurate hazard identification and safe processing during launch site processing activities. Brandl served as the Payload Safety Working Group chairman for numerous LSP missions (including Aeronomy of Ice in the Mesosphere satellite, Aquarius, JUNO and Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter/Lunar Crater Observation and Sensing Satellite), working with various NASA centers, as well as domestic and international partners.

Brandl spent the last seven years as the lead of the QE and Quality Assurance (QA) Branch for LSP. In this role, he provided leadership and technical expertise to a team of Quality engineers and QA specialist personnel responsible for assessing the effectiveness of Expendable Launch Vehicle (ELV) contractor’s Quality Management System, the health of the launch vehicle build processes and launch vehicle production supply chains. Brandl was responsible for managing and leading a large team of QA specialists at various commercial launch vehicle manufacturing and production locations, including multiple launch sites. Brandl has participated in numerous launch vehicle contractor and supplier AS9100 audits, and NASA-led audits during launch vehicle certification efforts.

During his time with the LSP SMA Division, he supported more than 40 LSP missions flying on various ELVs such as Delta II, Delta IV, Atlas V, Pegasus XL and Falcon 9.

Brandl has a bachelor’s degree in computer engineering from the University of Central Florida. He has been recognized with multiple center awards, including the NASA Silver Achievement Medal and the Kennedy Certificate of Appreciation.

Additive Manufacturing Product Qualification Initiatives

SMA-DFS-011
Print Version

As the development of custom-designed Additive Manufactured (AM) parts gains momentum in the United States and throughout the world, NASA and its space partners are looking to utilize these parts in critical aerospace applications due to the unique benefits they provide compared to traditional wrought and cast parts.

Exciting advantages of AM parts include significant weight savings, cost savings, shorter lead times, fewer welds, less scrap and enhanced function due to the ability to produce ‘design-to-constraint’ parts with complex geometries previously impossible to manufacture.

Although AM materials and process issues are being identified and resolved, many gaps remain in currently available standards and specifications for design, precursor and finished material properties, process control, post-processing, qualification and certification, testing, maintenance, and repair. These gaps need to be addressed in order to ensure Quality, Reliability and safety.

The production and qualification of AM parts require strict engineering standards and rigorous process controls to identify and address previously unknown material conditions and defects, particularly as these parts are used for the first time in critical space applications.

In addition to highlighting the latest AM work at NASA and elsewhere, the speakers provide insight into the challenges associated with AM from a Quality Assurance perspective. They also discuss the current initiatives being pursued by NASA and its space partners — industry, other government agencies and standards development organizations — to overcome these challenges. The discussion will also include essential qualification and certification steps drawn from NASA Marshall Space Flight Center’s Engineering and Quality Standard for Spaceflight Hardware.

Course Number: SMA-DFS-011

Length: 2.00 hours

Audience: This course is intended for Quality Assurance practitioners from across the agency.

Eric Burke

Nondestructive Evaluation Program Manager

Eric Burke is a research and development physicist at NASA Langley Research Center where he serves as the deputy Nondestructive Evaluation (NDE) Program manager for NASA’s Office of Safety and Mission Assurance.

With over 10 years of engineering experience within the NASA community, Burke focuses on the development and application of innovative materials and technologies. He is a Subject Matter Expert in the field of in-situ NDE on Additive Manufacturing (AM) parts and leads NDE activities and research on AM across all of the NASA centers.

Burke also leads the Small Business Innovative Research and Development program for technologies that will apply to future inspection of the International Space Station and deep space vehicles. Recent highlights include the delivery of a single side 3D x-ray system that is being used to scan the heat shield of the Orion spacecraft currently located at Kennedy Space Center. This prototype x-ray system is proving to be a critical piece for inspection of Avcoat® joints for the Orion program.

Burke has received national recognition for excellence in the area of NDE and technology development including the NASA Early Career Achievement Medal and Launch Director’s Award. He holds three U.S. patents and has authored or co-authored nearly 20 conference papers.

Prior to joining NASA in 2010, Burke worked as a research and development engineer for United Space Alliance and the University of Dayton.

Burke holds both a Bachelor of Science degree and a Master of Science degree in mechanical engineering, solid mechanics, from the University of Dayton.

Ed Generazio

Nondestructive Evaluation Program Manager

Ed Generazio serves as the program manager for the Office of Safety and Mission Assurance’s (OSMA) Nondestructive Evaluation (NDE) Program. The NDE Program addresses mission-critical inspection issues and has an immediate impact for the agency by assuring component integrity for the safety and reliability of NASA’s flight systems. Generazio’s current research focuses on establishing validated probability of detection processes, procedures and NDE standards for establishing the integrity of fracture-critical flight components.

Generazio has developed a new Electric Field Imaging (EFI) inspection technology for exposing hidden objects on personnel and underground and evaluating the integrity of tethers and wire insulation and material variations in structures. He currently is extending EFI work for applications in the medical imaging and organic memory systems fields. Generazio has produced hundreds of publications and has served as a keynote speaker at major professional conferences. He continually mentors junior staff across the agency to develop centers’ NDE leaders, and has received numerous awards, including NASA’s Outstanding Leadership Medal.

When NASA established a Headquarters-based Independent Technical Authority to oversee all of NASA’s programs in January 2005, Generazio was asked to serve as NASA’s technical warrant holder for NDE. As the NDE technical warrant holder, Generazio was the final authority to address issues affecting the safe and reliable operations of NASA’s numerous programs that range from aeronautics, launch vehicles, Space Shuttle, satellite systems and Space Station to deep space probes. With a considerable amount of mission-critical NDE activity in each of these areas, Generazio deputized NDE personnel as trusted agents to serve as his eyes and ears at NASA’s 10 centers. 

After the Columbia accident, Generazio served as the discipline expert for NDE for the NASA Safety and Engineering Center until 2006. As an NDE discipline expert, he guided the activities of 90 experts from industry, universities, other government agencies and NASA centers. These experts operated as an NDE Super Problem Resolution Team (SPRT) that worked in 10 groups on NASA’s unique critical inspection issues that required the attention of the "best of the best" in order to return to flight. For example, the NDE SPRT worked aggressively to develop and certify Nondestructive Inspection technologies for inspecting the integrity of sprayed-on foam insulation used to insulate the Space Transportation System’s external tank. 

Generazio received his doctorate in physics from The Pennsylvania State University in 1983. After graduation, he worked at NASA's Lewis Research Center (now Glenn Research Center) in Cleveland, Ohio, as a senior research scientist in NDE where he expanded his skills to develop a Technology Benefit Estimator that integrated engine and flight cycle simulations with component specific modeling, transonic flow, structural analyses, manufacturing processes and probabilistic analyses to estimate the value of new proposed technologies. After working as a researcher for 10 years, Generazio served as branch head of the NASA Langley Research Center Nondestructive Evaluation Sciences Branch (NESB) in Hampton, Virginia, and the delegated NDE program manager for OSMA. As manager of NESB, he was responsible for the development of advanced NDE and structural health monitoring technologies for the next generation of aerospace systems and aging systems.

Nondestructive Evaluation Development Program

Functions
Print Version

The Office of Safety and Mission Assurance's Nondestructive Evaluation (NDE) Development Program provides an agencywide focus and forum for the integration of major NDE program needs. The program

  • Promotes improvements in communication and cooperation throughout the NASA community by sponsoring meetings, workshops and other technical interchanges
  • Facilitates rapid agency-side information exchanges through the use of newsletters, electronic information and an NDE directory
  • Encourages inter-center and intra-center cooperative programs coupled with external organizations and technical societies through a Memoranda of Understanding
  • Promotes productivity and efficiency through sharing experiences, procedures, techniques, resources and efforts directed towards technology implementation
  • Fosters partnerships between program offices, engineering, Quality Assurance and the NASA NDE community
  • Provides an agency-wide forum for the sharing of NDE technology, including technology transfer within NASA and to other government and commercial agencies
  • Provides an interface with U.S. universities and industry as well as the international NDE community
  • Supports the premise that accurate and reliable NDE measurements are fundamental to the advancement of NASA's scientific and engineering programs
