Mishap Investigation

Mishap investigations allow NASA to understand the root cause of incidents and make adjustments to prevent recurrence. NASA conducts mishap investigations in accordance with NASA Procedural Requirements for Mishap Reporting, Investigating, and Recordkeeping (NPR 8621.1), which describes the procedure for reporting, investigating and documenting mishaps, close calls and previously unidentified workplace hazards to prevent similar occurrences in the future.

Policy and Guidance

NASA

NPR 8621.1 NASA Procedural Requirements for Mishap and Close Call Reporting, Investigating, and Recordkeeping

This policy provides requirements to report, investigate and document mishaps, close calls and previously unidentified serious workplace hazards to prevent recurrence of similar accidents.

See NPR 8621.1

NASA Mishap Information System

The NASA Mishap Information System (NMIS) is a custom-developed system for capturing mishaps, close calls and hazards, as required in NPR 8621.1, NASA Procedural Requirements for Mishap and Close Call Reporting, Investigating, and Recordkeeping.

Learn More About NMIS Log Into NMIS

Mishap Preparedness and Contingency Plan Software

The Mishap Preparedness and Contingency Plan (MPCP) software is designed to assist NASA programs and projects in developing Contingency Plans required under NPR 8621.1, NASA Procedural Requirements for Mishap and Close Call Reporting, Investigating, and Recordkeeping. Program and project required inputs relating to mission success, pre-declares, hardware, hazards and key process phases are captured in a structured format with the final product generated in Microsoft Word.

Log Into MPCP
 

NASA Cause Analysis Tool

The NASA Cause Analysis Tool (NCAT) is a custom-developed web application used to collaborate on root cause analyses (RCA). NCAT will support the full mishap investigation process as defined in NPR 8621.1, but also makes root cause analysis techniques more accessible for smaller mishap investigations and other use cases, such as assessments.

Learn More About NCAT Log Into NCAT

Columbia

On Feb. 1, 2003, after a successful 16-day mission, the Columbia and her crew — commander Rick Husband; pilot William McCool; mission specialists David Brown, Kalpana Chawla, Michael Anderson, and Laurel Clark; and payload specialist Ilan Ramon — headed back to Earth. Sixteen minutes from home, the space shuttle broke apart during re-entry into Earth's atmosphere. NASA, other organizations and the public immediately set out to find the crew and remaining pieces of Columbia.

While NASA needed to study Columbia to learn exactly what happened to the shuttle and return to flight, the agency also knew that the artifacts had a lot to teach others. NASA started the loan program, so others could study her: learning her lessons and advancing science for future generations. This is Columbia's continued mission.

Frequently Asked Questions

  • Who owns NPR 8621.1, NASA Procedural Requirements for Mishap and Close Call Reporting, Investigating, and Recordkeeping?
    The Office of Safety and Mission Assurance at NASA Headquarters, specifically the NASA mishap program executive, is the owner of this policy. The Mishap Investigation Support Office at the NASA Safety Center provides administrative, technical and analytical assistance to Mishap Investigation Boards and to the NASA mishap program executive.
  • Who owns the training and tools for Mishap Investigation?
    The Office of Safety and Mission Assurance at NASA Headquarters owns the training and tools.
  • Who implements the training for Mishap Investigation?
    The Office of Safety and Mission Assurance, with support from the NASA Safety Center, implements training. Supervisors, Safety and Mission Assurance directors, and safety managers should ensure there are sufficient personnel at their sites with the required training. “Introduction to Mishap Investigations” in SATERN is required for at least one board member and the ex-officio.
  • Is course work all it takes to be a “trained” investigator?
    No, but it doesn’t hurt. Having served or participated on a Mishap Investigation Board and having a thorough understanding of the NPR 8621.1 also is beneficial.
  • Who manages Mishap Investigation Boards?
    The chair appointed to the mishap investigation manages the board.
  • During an investigation, can you interview someone more than one time?
    Yes. As the investigation progresses, additional facts may warrant revisiting some key witnesses.
  • Can interviews be recorded?
    Yes. The witness must be made aware that he or she is being recorded and asked for consent. NPR 8621.1, Section 3.8 provides additional details on witness statements and confidentiality.
  • Is it permissible to ask about a person’s medical history?
    Yes, it is permissible to ask about a person’s medical history, but this information cannot be released.
  • Should direct quotes from an interviewee be included in the mishap report?
    No. Direct quotes could compromise the confidentiality of the witness.
  • Who can attend deliberations of a Mishap Investigation Board (MIB)?
    Participation is limited to NASA employees. All board members listed in the appointment letter should participate in deliberations. In addition, consultants do not participate in deliberations (vote on findings) (NPR 8621.1, paragraph 1.4.19 a).
  • Can a contractor support the creation of the Events and Causal Factor Tree (E&CFT)?
    In general, no. The E&CFT should be a product of the MIB, which is comprised of NASA personnel.
  • Why is a weather event classified separately from earthquakes, volcanoes, etc.?
    Earthquakes and volcanoes generally are considered to be of a higher severity and of less common frequency than normal or expected seasonal changes in weather.
  • Under what circumstances would upper management change a mishap classification?
    Property damage estimates could change and require a change in classification. Also, a high-visibility incident may require an increase.
  • For injuries, why are workdays, not weekdays counted?
    This requirement comes from the Occupational Safety and Health Administration listed in 1904.7(b)(3)(iv): “How do I count weekends, holidays, or other days the employee would not have worked anyway? You must count the number of calendar days the employee was unable to work as a result of the injury or illness, regardless of whether or not the employee was scheduled to work on those day(s). Weekend days, holidays, vacation days or other days off are included in the total number of days recorded if the employee would not have been able to work on those days because of a work-related injury or illness.”
  • Does the Office of Safety and Mission Assurance (OSMA) verify contractor participation in mishap investigations for a given contract?
    There is an ongoing effort to include such language in contracts for NASA contractors.
  • Does the NASA Safety Center (NSC) approve the final mishap report?
    No. Approval of final mishap reports and products is the responsibility of the appointing and endorsing officials.
  • What is the notification process for mishaps? Can it be informal?
    The notification for all classifications of mishaps and close calls is described in NPR 8621.1, Chapter 3, Initial Response to a Mishap or Close Call.
  • Do all advisors have to be used in an investigation? Do they all sign the report?
    If an advisor is listed in the appointment letter, his or her signature also must appear on the final report. Additional support personnel should be acknowledged.
  • Is there any plan to have more than three Mishap Investigation specialists for the agency?
    This is being discussed, and the Mishap Investigation Support Office and the agency mishap manager welcome involvement and comment from the mishap community.
  • Do all levels of mishaps (Types A, B, C, D, Close Call) require the same techniques or tools during the investigation, e.g., Root Cause Analysis and E&CFT?
    The same techniques and tools can be used for all mishaps. Root Cause Analysis is necessary for all mishaps, but not all elements of the analysis are required to be in the final report.
  • Do all mishap investigations find a root cause?
    No, but they need to attempt to determine if any exist.
  • Can a contributing factor have a root cause?
    Yes, but it is a root cause for the contributing factor and should be listed as such.
  • Who approves Center Mishap Preparedness and Contingency Plans?
    The chief, OSMA approves Center Mishap Preparedness and Contingency Plans.

David Erickson

Mishap Program specialist, ACCLLP Program

David Erickson is a mishap program specialist in the NASA Safety Center’s Mishap Prevention and Organizational Performance (MPOP) Office. In this role, he supports NASA mishap investigations and assists with the development of the new Columbia Learning Center at Kennedy Space Center, along with providing future training opportunities. He is located at Kennedy Space Center.

Erickson’s mishap experience includes serving on the NASA Standing Accident Investigation Boards (SAIBs) for multiple high-visibility mishaps. He also served on the Columbia Reconstruction Project at the Reusable Launch Vehicle Hangar.

Erickson is a six-year veteran of the U.S. Navy where he specialized in the nuclear power field. He worked on NASA’s Space Shuttle Program for 11 years and started as an industrial engineer in the United Space Alliance (USA) Industrial Engineering and Human Factors group.

He contributed to the Lockheed Martin/United Space Alliance team on the winning Crew Exploration Vehicle proposal (which became Orion). He also served as the launch operations safety manager for the United States’ Return to Flight missions in 2005 and 2006. During the closeout of the Space Shuttle Program, Erickson transitioned to the role of ground systems reliability manager while continuing to support pad launch preparations and post-launch safe-ing through the launch of STS 133 in February of 2011.

After serving with the Shuttle Program, Erickson was the safety manager for a Missile Defense Agency (MDA) contractor and provided systems safety for vehicle and delivery design, test and flight of MDA targets. He also worked in occupational safety to ensure compliance with the Florida Department of Environmental Protection for multiple L3 companies in the Orlando metro area until his company was purchased by Aerojet Rocketdyne.

In 2019, Erickson returned to Kennedy Space Center as a contractor and earned a Silver Snoopy Award for activities involving the testing and fueling of Orion at the Multi-Payload Processing Facility for Artemis I in 2022. He also served as the operations lead in the Exploration Ground Systems Safety Division for Artemis processing.

Erickson earned his Certified Safety Professional designation from the Board of Certified Safety Professionals. He has a bachelor’s degree in industrial engineering and a master's degree in industrial safety (now called environmental safety and health).

A Minnesota native, Erickson has lived in Florida since 2000. He is married and has two adult children who are world travelers.

Masters also has experience performing technical and nontechnical engineering evaluations in the form of independent assessments relating to SMA for internal and external customers, including launch vehicle processing, the International Space Station, Constellation program, Launch Services Program and Kennedy institutional facilities.

    Mishap Classifications

    Print Version

    Classification Level

    Property Damage

    Injury

    Type A Mishap

    Total direct cost of mission failure and property damage of $2,000,000 or more,

    or

    Crewed aircraft hull loss,

    or

    Unexpected aircraft departure from controlled flight for all aircraft except when departure from controlled flight has been pre-briefed (e.g., upset recovery training, high AOA envelope testing, aerobatics, or OCF for training) or mitigated through the flight test process inherent at each Center.

    Occupational injury or illness resulting in

    A fatality,

    or

    A permanent total disability.

    Type B Mishap

    Total direct cost of mission failure and property damage of at least $500,000, but less than $2,000,000.

    Occupational injury or illness resulting in

    A permanent partial disability,

    or

    Hospitalization for inpatient care of three or more people within 30 workdays of the mishap.

    Type C Mishap

    Total direct cost of mission failure and property damage of at least $50,000, but less than $500,000.

    Nonfatal OSHA-recordable occupational injury or illness resulting in days away from work, or restricted duty, or transfer to another job beyond the day or shift on which it occurred.

    or

    Hospitalization for inpatient care of one or two people within 30 workdays of the mishap.

    Type D Mishap

    Total direct cost of mission failure and property damage of at least $20,000, but less than $50,000.

    Nonfatal OSHA-recordable occupational injury or illness that does not meet the definition of a Type C mishap.

    Close Call

    Total direct cost of mission failure and property damage of less than $20,000, but event has the mishap potential using a worst case estimate.

    Injury requiring first aid or less, but event has the mishap potential using a worst case estimate.

    NASA Interim Response Team Training

    SMA-002-11
    Print Version

    The primary role of the Interim Response Team (IRT) is to preserve evidence for the Investigating Authority, who will determine the cause of the mishap. “NASA Interim Response Team Training” is an agency-level introductory course that is intended to supplement center- and program-specific IRT training. The course explains NASA mishap response policies and procedures, how NASA prepares to respond to a mishap, and the roles and responsibilities of key players in a mishap response. The course also provides an overview of what an IRT member needs to know to secure a mishap scene, document the scene and preserve evidence.  

    Course Number:  SMA-002-11

    Length:  5.00 hours

    Audience: This course is intended for personnel who have been appointed to serve on an IRT or who support mishap investigations at their center or for their program.

    Introduction to Human Factors in Mishap and Close Call Investigation

    SMA-001-07
    Print Version

    This course provides an introduction to the basic terms and concepts associated with human factors in Mishap and Close Call Investigation. It defines and explores examples of human events and conditions that cause and contribute to mishaps and close calls. It distinguishes between the four types of human events: 1) correct action, 2) no action, 3) incorrect actions (errors and unsafe acts), and 4) physical or physiological failures and changes in state. This course describes four types of human errors (perception, interpretation, decision making and action execution). It discusses both internal and external performance shaping factors and their contribution to mishaps and close calls. Additionally, it describes barriers, controls and amelioration that can be used to reduce the risks associated with human errors and unsafe acts.

    Course Number: SMA-001-07

    Length: 3.00 hours

    Audience: This course is intended for mishap investigators, human factors engineers, safety professionals, risk managers and management.
