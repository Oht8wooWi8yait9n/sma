Subdisciplines

Learn more about the series of subdisciplines that Institutional Safety oversees.

Construction Safety

Construction exposes workers to many hazards and is the highest-risk activity occurring at NASA centers, and falls are one of the top causes of fatalities in construction.

The program aims to prevent accidents and keep agency personnel and contractors safe. NASA Construction Safety and Fall Prevention professionals review applicable policies, share Construction Safety concerns, and identify and create practices to resolve those concerns.

The program also works to incorporate into NASA construction projects a relatively new idea in the safety arena — prevention through design. The root of this philosophy is to construct new facilities so that fall protection is never, or at least rarely, necessary. This is achieved through methods such as installing a proper parapet around the roof so that when maintenance or inspections occur there is no need for a full body harness, lanyard, anchor point or connecting device.

Control of Hazardous Energy (Lockout/Tagout)

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration defines hazardous energy as “Energy sources including electrical, mechanical, hydraulic, pneumatic, chemical, thermal or other sources in machines and equipment…” To prevent injury or death, it is essential to control hazardous energy when performing work.

Lockout/Tagout procedures protect employees from hazardous energy. Locks are used to isolate physically a piece of machinery or equipment, while tags inform affected employees about the isolation. Every NASA center and facility has specific Lockout/Tagout procedures to protect employees.

Electrical Safety

The NASA Electrical Safety program is designed to prevent electrically related injuries and property damage. This program also provides for proper training of employees to ensure they have the requisite knowledge and understanding of electrical work practices and procedures.

Emergency Management and COOP

Emergency Management and Continuity of Operations Plans (COOPs) are guided by requirements in NPR 8715.2, NPR 1040.1 and NPD 1040.4. It is NASA policy to ensure that the agency maintains a comprehensive and effective continuity capability composed of programs designed to ensure the continuous performance of essential functions under all conditions. Furthermore, the agency must have emergency management programs, plans and procedures that work within the protocol of the National Response Framework and the National Incident Management System.

Facility System Safety

Facility System Safety applies a System Safety approach to the life cycle of facilities. It’s more than design and construction — it involves using System Safety logic to approach safety and creates a way to provide configuration control for facilities' safe operation.

Fall Protection

NASA requires all centers to implement a Fall Protection Program and appoint a center Fall Protection Program administrator to develop, implement and manage the program. The program focuses on eliminating, mitigating and controlling fall hazards before an individual is exposed.

Fire Protection

NASA’s Fire Protection Program oversees fire prevention, detection, control and suppression through engineering, inspection, training and firefighting. The program aims to protect human life, property and the environment from fire-related hazards.

Hazardous Materials

According to NPR 8715.3, Section 3.7 — NASA General Safety Program Requirements, Hazardous Material Transportation, Storage, and Use — NASA is responsible for protecting persons, property and the environment from exposure during the transportation, storage and use of hazardous materials.

Hazardous materials are substances or materials in a quantity and form that could pose an unreasonable risk to health, safety or property when transported in commerce. Typical hazardous materials are those that may be highly reactive, toxic, poisonous, explosive, flammable, combustible and corrosive; produce contamination or pollution of the environment; or cause adverse health effects or unsafe conditions. Materials include nanomaterials, cryogens, and hazardous material leaks or spills.

Hazardous Operations

Hazardous Operations refers to any operation without implementation of proper mitigations or that has a high potential to result in the loss of life, serious injury to personnel or public, or damage to property as a result of the material or equipment involved or the nature of the operation or activity itself. Hazardous Operations is covered in NPR 8715.3, Chapter 3.

More

Laboratory Safety

Laboratory Safety, covered in NPR 8715.3, Chapter 3, is responsible for protecting personnel and property in lab environments. This discipline also includes certain equipment, repair and calibration operations, as well as processing of materials.

More

Mishap and Close Call Reporting, Investigating and Recordkeeping

Mishap investigations allow NASA to understand the root cause of incidents and make adjustments to prevent recurrence. NASA conducts mishap investigations in accordance with NASA Procedural Requirements for Mishap Reporting, Investigating, and Recordkeeping (NPR 8621.1), which describes the procedure for reporting, investigating and documenting mishaps, close calls and previously unidentified workplace hazards to prevent similar occurrences in the future.

Motor Vehicle Safety

It is NASA’s responsibility to ensure that motor vehicle operations comply with federal, state and local regulations. To do so, the agency implements continuous duty time for operators, policies that ban handheld communication devices while driving and require the use of seatbelts, formal operator training, vehicle safety inspections, and traffic control devices on NASA property. Although Motor Vehicle Safety covers bicycles and powered carts, it does not apply to aircraft, spacecraft, vessels, watercraft or lifting devices that are in motion while completing a lift.

More

Occupational Safety

Occupational Safety requirements in NPR 8715.1 ensure that employees are furnished with a place of employment that is free from recognized hazards that cause or are likely to cause death or serious harm. It is NASA policy to implement a comprehensive Occupational Safety and Health program consistent with the OSHA Act of 1970, Executive Order 12196 and 29 CFR 1960.

More

Operational Safety

Operational Safety focuses on the prevention of operations-related safety hazards. It includes all areas of industrial or occupational safety traditionally found within the industry, and each of these areas are governed within Chapter 3 of NPR 8715.3C, NASA General Safety Program Requirements.

More

Personal Protective Equipment

NASA is required to provide Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) to all NASA employees in situations where engineering controls, management controls or other corrective actions have not reduced the hazard to an acceptable level or where use of engineering controls, management controls or other techniques is not feasible. Protective equipment, including PPE for eyes, face, head and extremities; protective clothing; respiratory devices; and protective shields and barriers are to be provided, used and maintained in a sanitary and reliable condition wherever it is necessary by reason of hazards of processes or environment, chemical hazards, radiological hazards or mechanical irritants encountered in a manner capable of causing injury or impairment in the function of any part of the body through absorption, inhalation or physical contact.

Center directors must ensure non-NASA, contractor and non-contractor personnel at their centers provide the appropriate training, fit testing and compliance with other federal, state, local and NASA PPE requirements.

More

Product Safety

Product Safety ensures the safety of products in their intended applications through product stewardship programs and compliance with requirements.

Safety Training and Certification

Safety Training and Certification oversees requirements for establishing safety training programs and the minimum training certification levels necessary for personnel involved in potentially hazardous NASA operations. Much of this training is available online. Instructor-based courses are available through the NASA Safety Training Center and SATERN.

More

Test Operations Safety

Test Operations Safety manages requirements for protecting personnel and property during test operations, for both human-controlled and unoccupied or robotic tests. Testing includes hazardous training activities and demonstrations of test hardware or procedures. The requirements apply to test facilities, test equipment located within or attached to test facilities, equipment being tested, test personnel, test conduct, and test documents.

More

Underwater Operations

Underwater Operations pertains to NASA neutral buoyancy facilities, equipment, personnel and operations involving underwater activities, including the simulation of a weightless environment and NASA personnel participating in underwater operations at non-NASA facilities. It also pertains to vessels, watercraft and submersible vehicles.

More

NASA Root Cause Analysis

SMA-002-14
Print Version

This course provides training on Root Cause Analysis (RCA) methodology that can be used in both general problem solving and mishap and close call investigations. It teaches the Root Cause Analysis methodology and terminology adopted by NASA in August 2003. It allows students to demonstrate their understanding of concepts and tools through examples and exercises, including differentiating between events and conditions, identifying cause and effect relationships, discriminating between barriers and controls, constructing a time line, RCA Fault Tree and Event and Causal Factor Trees. 

Course Number:  SMA-002-14

Length:  16 hours

Audience: Those who are involved with mishap investigations, including NASA Mishap Investigation Working Group members, mishap investigators, mishap investigation board members, and incident response team members. It also includes engineers, managers, quality assurance professionals and problem solvers.

David Erickson

Mishap Program specialist, ACCLLP Program

David Erickson is a mishap program specialist in the NASA Safety Center’s Mishap Prevention and Organizational Performance (MPOP) Office. In this role, he supports NASA mishap investigations and assists with the development of the new Columbia Learning Center at Kennedy Space Center, along with providing future training opportunities. He is located at Kennedy Space Center.

Erickson’s mishap experience includes serving on the NASA Standing Accident Investigation Boards (SAIBs) for multiple high-visibility mishaps. He also served on the Columbia Reconstruction Project at the Reusable Launch Vehicle Hangar.

Erickson is a six-year veteran of the U.S. Navy where he specialized in the nuclear power field. He worked on NASA’s Space Shuttle Program for 11 years and started as an industrial engineer in the United Space Alliance (USA) Industrial Engineering and Human Factors group.

He contributed to the Lockheed Martin/United Space Alliance team on the winning Crew Exploration Vehicle proposal (which became Orion). He also served as the launch operations safety manager for the United States’ Return to Flight missions in 2005 and 2006. During the closeout of the Space Shuttle Program, Erickson transitioned to the role of ground systems reliability manager while continuing to support pad launch preparations and post-launch safe-ing through the launch of STS 133 in February of 2011.

After serving with the Shuttle Program, Erickson was the safety manager for a Missile Defense Agency (MDA) contractor and provided systems safety for vehicle and delivery design, test and flight of MDA targets. He also worked in occupational safety to ensure compliance with the Florida Department of Environmental Protection for multiple L3 companies in the Orlando metro area until his company was purchased by Aerojet Rocketdyne.

In 2019, Erickson returned to Kennedy Space Center as a contractor and earned a Silver Snoopy Award for activities involving the testing and fueling of Orion at the Multi-Payload Processing Facility for Artemis I in 2022. He also served as the operations lead in the Exploration Ground Systems Safety Division for Artemis processing.

Erickson earned his Certified Safety Professional designation from the Board of Certified Safety Professionals. He has a bachelor’s degree in industrial engineering and a master's degree in industrial safety (now called environmental safety and health).

A Minnesota native, Erickson has lived in Florida since 2000. He is married and has two adult children who are world travelers.

    NASA Efforts Towards a Holistic COTS Approach for Space Avionics

    Print Version

    Aerospace electrical hardware systems are required to reliably operate in extremely harsh environments that include, for example, temperature extremes, intense vibrational stress, drastic temperature cycling and high radiation exposure. The nature of aerospace electronics also demands high initial reliability since, with very few exceptions, there is no chance for servicing and repairs. Common risk mitigation techniques for this type of situation are to use electrical components that are regarded as “high reliability” because of additional controls and requirements applied in their design, manufacturing and testing. That methodology has been successful for decades, but limited availability of space-grade MIL-SPEC parts and a need for using the most current technologies in system designs have necessitated the incorporation of commercial electronics into space flight hardware.

    This presentation presents the methodology championed at NASA to utilize more commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) electronics. This includes fostering relationships with high-quality manufacturers who are producing very reliable devices in high volume, using automated production equipment and statistical process controls to establish “zero defect” workmanship. After verification of these criteria, these companies are designated as “Industry Leading Parts Manufacturers (ILPMs).” Additional testing may also be needed to ensure the parts meet the project’s Mission, Environment, Application and Lifetime (MEAL), including radiation testing.

Using off-the-shelf software from the NASA provided Microsoft Suite (Power Automate, PowerBI, and SharePoint) and free data analysis source code from Python, RCode, and Julia the team was able to collect, process, and analyze various launch vehicle databases (non-conformances, design changes, supplier management, risk, manufacturing key characteristics, etc.) to highlight focus areas for the mission assurance team.  

    Using Microsoft Power Automate, the team was able to automate the data gathering of hundreds of thousands of data points and provide a method to regularly update data week-to-week. Once data model was established linking data from multiple sources, the team used Microsoft PowerBI to display the information and publish to SharePoint as dynamic dashboards for NASA Mission Assurance team. By manipulating the filters on the dashboard, team members can focus attention in a proactive versus reactive manner by focusing on variations in work centers, site locations, particular manufacturing and testing processes, non-conformance disposition and/or a flight critical component. Armed with the data, NASA personnel can use the time series data and statistics to investigate further into potential problem areas and focus their attention on reducing mission risk. 

    In addition to focusing on fleet-wide trends, the team has used the information to improve the internal processes for Hardware Acceptance Review (HAR), Configuration Management (CM) and Supplier Management Review (SMR). With limited personnel resources, the team uses the data collected to selects HAR components specific to the mission.  Using PowerBI and Power Automate, the team can recommend the flight critical components needing review by the mission team based on criteria such as high number of non-conformances, design changes, first flight items, testing anomalies, and/or identified risks. Furthermore, by running the Power Automate scripts multiple times a week the team can monitor the launch vehicle configuration and inform team members when a component has been installed on the vehicle. In turn, associated Work Orders (WOs) and Acceptance Data Packages (ADPs) are ready for review by the assigned mission assurance team member. To monitor the teams progress during the HAR, PowerBI dashboards allow for a quick graphical review by leadership. 

    Just like how the improvements in the HAR process focus the team on what to review and when to review it, the improvements in the Supplier Management Review increased team efficiency. Using the data model, the team can narrow down focus on suppliers needing more attention and provide recommendations on the launch provider supplier audits the NASA SMA should participate in throughout the year. What use to be a manual process, the team applied the same criteria established from the Kaizen event held a few years ago to the data model resulting in an automated go-no go decision. The team can focus resources on struggling suppliers and monitor the overall health of the suppliers. 

    With the number of launch providers and launched increasing each year, NASA LSP SMA will continue to look for new ways to improve efficiency. Python, RCode, and Juila interface with PowerBI is being used to improve the graphical presentation and interaction for the user as well as assessing the time series for predictive and statistical analysis. For example, using functions within RCode the time series data for the rocket engine inlet pressure or weekly non-conformance tickets can be broken down into trend, seasonality, and randomness components to provide a clearer picture of data and highlight areas of concern. 

    Ultimately, NASA LSP SMA is working to improve their internal processes to reduce manual tasks while improving on the decision-making tools. In an environment with strained resources and increasing workloads, the team has found innovative ways to us the NASA standard software tools provided to them in order to increase efficiency of the mission assurance team.

    Challenging the role of Quality Engineering in NASA projects

    Print Version

    Challenging the role of Quality Engineering in NASA projects: Maximizing Our Value and Achieving Industry Alignment 

    Traditionally, quality engineering in NASA projects has limited involvement during the early phases of development. This is largely due to the perception that quality engineers are only responsible for inspection and requirements verification, coupled with early-stage budget constraints and a lack of awareness within the engineering and project management communities of the value quality engineers can add. As a result, projects prioritize proof of concept and technology maturation over allocating even minimal resources to early quality engineering, despite its potential for high return on investment. This delayed involvement often forces quality engineers to overcompensate later, addressing risks and missed opportunities that could have been mitigated during product development or supplier selection. 

    In contrast, the aerospace industry views quality engineering as integral throughout the entire product lifecycle, starting with new product development. Industry quality engineers take on a broader role, implementing Advanced Product Quality Planning (APQP) tools early in the process to identify and mitigate risks. As projects progress, they also lead continuous improvement efforts and are accountable for process performance metrics during manufacturing, ensuring that quality is embedded from the outset and that lessons learned are continuously applied to achieve zero waste. 

    NASA’s current approach often cites the unique, low-volume nature of its product portfolio, implying that broader industry practices are not applicable. However, in an environment of tight budgets and frequent project cancellations, NASA’s quality engineering community must rethink its role in helping projects stay on budget and on schedule. This presentation will examine the benefits of involving quality engineering early for NASA projects. It will also present a case study highlighting pilot metrics designed to measure execution success of inhouse projects. In addition, recommendations to better educate our community on how quality engineering not only drives tangible improvements but also ensures proper recognition for these contributions will be outlined.

    NASA Range Flight Safety Analysis

    Print Version

    Range flight safety analysis is a comprehensive analysis aimed at demonstrating that the risks to public, NASA workforce, and any property requiring protection posed by hazards from NASA range operations associated with flight are acceptable.


    Purpose

    Each range operation shall undergo a range safety analysis to establish any design or operational constraints needed to control risk to persons and property.

    Requirements

    Incorporate the elements of risk management, risk assessment, risk mitigation and/or containment.

    Individual Risk

    • The Probability of Casualty (PC) for Mission Essential, Critical Operations, and Neighboring Operations Personnel (NOP) shall be less than or equal to (≤) 10 × 10-6.
    • The PC for the public shall be less than or equal to (≤) 1 × 10-6.

    Collective Risk

    • The Expectation of Casualty (EC) for the combination of Mission Essential, Critical Operations, and NOP shall be less than or equal to (≤) 300 × 10-6.
    • The Ec for the Public shall be less than or equal to (≤) 100 × 10-6.

     

    Property Risk

    • The Probability of Impact (PI) for any property and damage of concern identified shall be less than or equal to (≤) 1 × 10-3, applied for each flight.


    Tasks Typically Performed by Range Flight Safety Analysis

    Assist in risk acceptance and mitigation decisions that integrate concerns for all identified hazards posed by the range flight operation.

    • Flight Safety System Needs Determination
    • Identify and Analyze Hazards 
      • Debris, blast, toxic, and other hazards as applicable
    • Risk Mitigation
      • Establish any design or operational constraints, e.g. hazard areas, needed to control hazards and risks to life and property based on containment and/or risk analysis
    • Risk Assessments
      • Individual risk (PC), Expected Casualty (EC), and Probability of Impact (PI)
    • Operational Support
      • Develop (in concert with RSO) Range Safety Displays and Range Safety Mission/Flight Rules
      • Monitoring of real-time hazards and associated risk

    Techniques

    For most programs, both containment and risk management are used.

    • Hazard Containment
      • Establish a large area such that all impacting pieces (debris) associated with that vehicle remain inside the designated hazard area.
      • Keeping all people and highly valued assets outside the designated impact area.
    • Safety Risk Management
      • Managing the residual risk due to any hazard not fully contained.
      • Determines probability of ship and/or aircraft impact.
      • Determines Individual Probability of Casualty (PC), Expected Casualty (EC) and Probability of Impact (PI) then compares to established criteria
    • Hazards considered are:
      • Direct impact of debris on people, structures, aircraft, ships, and boats
      • Blast Hazards (both near field and distant focusing overpressure)
      • Toxic Hazards
      • Other Hazards as applicable

    Flight Safety Systems

    Print Version

    Purpose

    Provides a means of preventing a launch or aeronautical vehicle and its hazards, including any payload hazards, from reaching any populated or other protected area in the event of a vehicle failure.

    Flight Termination System (FTS)

    • A capability designed, tested and incorporated into vehicles that provides for the independent and deliberate termination of an errant or erratic vehicle
    • Required for each propulsive stage
    • Specific design dependent on type of vehicle and mission

    Tracking System

    • Must provide continuous vehicle position and status during launch, flight and entry to the point where the public is no longer at risk
    • Two valid and independent data sources must be provided (e.g. 2 radars, 1 radar and telemetry, 1 radar and optics, etc.)

    Telemetry System

    • Must provide continuous flight safety related data during pre-launch operations, lift-off and throughout the time that the flight safety system is required to protect public areas
    • A telemetry receiving system must have the capacity to acquire, store, process, and provide real-time data to the RSO for the purpose of supporting a flight termination decision

    Contingency Management or Recovery System (Applies to UAVs and Reusable Vehicles)

    • Saves or preserves the flight vehicle in the event of a malfunction
    • Not a FTS – must be independent of the FTS
    • Can be considered as risk mitigation and factor into the safety risk assessment

    Range Flight Operations

    Print Version

    The use of specific infrastructure as well as trained and certified human interfaces to monitor, command and control the range safety elements associated with program flight/launch operations.

    Purpose

    To ensure hazard areas for flight and launch activities are implemented to satisfy the range’s risk criteria.

    Range Surveillance
    The agreements, procedures, and instrumentation system used during the countdown to ensure range’s land, sea and airspace are clear for launch.

    Countdown Procedures
    Provides orderly, step-by-step scripting to reduce the amount of real-time judgement required.

    Launch/Flight Commit Criteria and Mission Rules
    Criteria and/or rules that must be met prior to final approval to launch or flight.

    Tasks Typically Performed by Range Safety Operations

    • Hazard Areas
      • Publicize and implement hazard areas (NOTAM/NOTMAR)
      • Provide surveillance
      • Airspace, marine and space (collision avoidance)
    • Conducts launch countdown
    • Establishes range safety launch/flight commit criteria and/or mission rules
    • Help establish weather launch/flight commit criteria

    Range Safety Officer functions
    For example, during ELV launches, the RSO can take flight termination action if there is:
    – Violation of established flight safety criteria (destruct line violation)
    – Obvious erratic flight (e.g. tumbling vehicle)
    – Unknown performance (e.g. loss of vehicle data)

    5.39 Exceptions for Model Rocket Motors

    5.39.1 Applicability

    This section shall apply to the subset of model rocket motors that satisfy all of the following:

    1. Ignited by electrical means using the manufacturer’s intended ignition system(s).
    2. Contain no more than 2.2 ounces (62.5 grams) of propellant material.
    3. Produce less than 17.92 pounds seconds (80 newton-seconds) of total impulse with thrust duration not less than 0.050 second.
    4. Constructed such that all the chemical ingredients are preloaded into a cylindrical paper or similarly constructed nonmetallic tube that will not fragment into sharp, hard pieces.
    5. Designed so they will not burst under normal conditions of use, are incapable of spontaneous ignition, and do not contain any type of explosive or pyrotechnic warhead other than a parachute or recovery-system activation charge.
    6. Used in the manner for which the manufacturer designed it (e.g. only in model rockets, not as an igniter for NASA experimental testing).
    7. Manufactured and certified in accordance with NFPA 1125 chapter 8.

    Any model rocket motor that does not satisfy these requirements shall adhere to the requirements for explosives in this standard.

    5.39.2 Storage

    5.39.2.1  Model rocket motors shall be treated and stored as Class 4.1 flammable materials in accordance with the packaging requirements in Department of Transportation Special Permit 7887 (DOT-SP 7887).

    5.39.2.2  The minimum storage requirements of applicable model rocket motors shall entail placement inside a flammable storage cabinet. 

    5.39.2.3  Model rocket motors shall not be stored with ignitors installed.

    5.39.3 Licensing

    Explosive licensing requirements of this document do not apply.

    5.39.4 Transportation

    5.39.4.1  Model rocket motors transported by vehicles shall comply with packaging specifications defined by Department of Transportation Special Permit 7887 (DOT-SP 7887). 

    5.39.4.2  Transportation of model rocket motors shall be done by an individual of at least 18 years of age trained in the hazards associated with model rocket motors. 

    5.39.5 Use

    The installation and use of model rocket motors shall conform to all local, state, and federal regulations in addition to the manufacturer’s instructions.

    5.39.6 Training and Medical Requirements

    When using model rocket motors applicable to section 5.39, individuals shall be exempt from the training and physical requirements of this document.

    Probabilistic Risk Assessment (PRA): Analytical Process for Recognizing Design and Operational Risk

    SMA-RM-WBT-251
    Print Version

    This course provides an overview of Probabilistic Risk Assessment (PRA), one of the tools in NASA’s Safety and Mission Assurance Toolbox. PRA is a comprehensive, structured and disciplined approach to identifying and analyzing risk in engineered systems and/or processes. It attempts to quantify rare event probabilities of failures. It attempts to take into account all possible events or influences that could reasonably affect the system or process being studied. It is inherently and philosophically a Bayesian methodology. In general, PRA is a process that seeks answers to three basic questions:

    1. What kinds of events or scenarios can occur (i.e., what can go wrong)?
    2. What are the likelihoods and associated uncertainties of the events or scenarios?
    3. What consequences could result from these events or scenarios?

    Course Number: SMA-RM-WBT-251

    Quality

    Print Version

    NASA uses both NASA-internal and internationally recognized standard requirements documents that drive higher compliance and lower cost through their technical applicability to NASA’s work, their technical validity, their repeatability and their stability. NPR 8735.2, NASA Hardware Quality Assurance Program Requirements for Programs and Projects, documents NASA’s Quality Assurance technical standards.

    NASA’s Office of Safety and Mission Assurance (OSMA) gives particular attention to leading and informing the hardware Quality policy in the following Quality sub-disciplines: Metrology and Calibration; Electrical and Mechanical Systems Workmanship; ESD Control; NDE; and Electrical, Electronic, and Electromechanical (EEE) Parts. These sub-disciplines are led by an OSMA-delegated program manager or a Subject Matter Expert. Typically, the sub-discipline leader heads up an agencywide working group, which represents NASA’s interests within the industry standards voluntary consensus community, and coordinates and recommends NASA Quality standards and/or policies. (See the EEE Parts page for information on OSMA’s program for Quality Assurance policy for EEE parts.)

    For mission hardware built by an external supplier, NASA Quality involves Government Contract Quality Assurance (GCQA) activities associated with contract administration. Federal Acquisition Regulations standardize contract clauses and inform contracting officers how to write contracts and then administer them throughout the life of the contract. NASA’s Quality Assurance Subject Matter Experts provide consultation to contract officers to ensure the right Quality requirements are included in the contracts and that the contracts provide for access by NASA Quality personnel to perform pre-acceptance verification of both the likelihood of a supplier to achieve compliance with the requirements and verification of the manufactured or processed items themselves. Product verifications may be performed during manufacturing (i.e., “in process”) or when processing is completed (i.e., “at acceptance”). GCQA requirements are found in NPR 8735.2.

    Occupational Safety and Health Administration statistics show that 40 percent of all construction-related injuries and deaths are caused by being struck by, caught in or caught between equipment or objects.     Owen Greulich

    Lifting Devices and Equipment Program Manager

    Owen Greulich is a mechanical engineer currently employed as Pressure Vessels and Pressurized Systems manager and Lifting Devices and Equipment manager in the Office of Safety and Mission Assurance at NASA Headquarters. His current responsibilities include safety of pressure and vacuum systems, while prior work has included project management of pressure systems design and implementation, major wind tunnel modifications, fabrication of research hardware, and construction of test stands.

    Greulich is responsible for development and quality of a number of internal standards, including review of standards in a wide range of engineering fields. He is active on the High Pressure Task Group of the American Society of Mechanical Engineers’ (ASME) Process Piping Code Committee and American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronautics (AIAA) committees on aerospace pressure systems standards. He is chair of the Fatigue and Fracture subcommittee for aerospace pressure vessels.

    Prior to his employment at NASA, Greulich worked in the private sector, designing and managing construction of pressure and vacuum systems and other specialized fabricated and machined hardware. Publications include work on integrity of wiring in aerospace applications, studies regarding rocket propellant safety and composite structures, and a book regarding engineering standards.

    Greulich received his Master of Engineering degree in Mechanical Engineering at the University of California in 1979. He is a registered Professional Engineer in the State of California.

    NASA Interim Response Team Training

    SMA-002-11
    Print Version

    The primary role of the Interim Response Team (IRT) is to preserve evidence for the Investigating Authority, who will determine the cause of the mishap. “NASA Interim Response Team Training” is an agency-level introductory course that is intended to supplement center- and program-specific IRT training.     Introduction to Human Factors in Mishap and Close Call Investigation

    SMA-001-07
    Print Version

    This course provides an introduction to the basic terms and concepts associated with human factors in Mishap and Close Call Investigation. It defines and explores examples of human events and conditions that cause and contribute to mishaps and close calls. It distinguishes between the four types of human events: 1) correct action, 2) no action, 3) incorrect actions (errors and unsafe acts), and 4) physical or physiological failures and changes in state. This course describes four types of human errors (perception, interpretation, decision making and action execution). It discusses both internal and external performance shaping factors and their contribution to mishaps and close calls. Additionally, it describes barriers, controls and amelioration that can be used to reduce the risks associated with human errors and unsafe acts.

    Course Number: SMA-001-07

    Length: 3.00 hours

    Audience: This course is intended for mishap investigators, human factors engineers, safety professionals, risk managers and management.

    4:1 Test Accuracy Ratio for Compliance with ANSI/NCSL Z540.1-1994 (R2002) Sub-Clause 10.2 B

    SMA-HQ-WBT-217
    Print Version

    ANSI/NCSL Z540.1-1994 (R2002), Calibration Laboratories and Measuring and Test Equipment – General Requirements [Z540.1] includes a requirement for calibration procedures to have a minimum Test Accuracy Ratio (TAR) of 4:1. This course includes an overview of the TAR, the formulas for calculating a TAR and examples of TAR calculations.
